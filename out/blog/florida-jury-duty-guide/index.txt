After helping families relocate to Tampa Bay for over two decades, I've fielded countless questions about jury duty in the Sunshine State. Let me walk you through exactly what to expect.</p>
<p>Florida takes jury duty seriously, but the process is more straightforward than most transplants fear. Whether you're coming from a state with dramatically different procedures or you've never served before, understanding Florida's system will save you stress when that summons arrives.</p>
<h2>How Florida's Jury Selection Actually Works</h2>
<p>Florida operates on a "one day, one trial" system in most counties. This means if you're not selected for a jury by the end of your first day, you're done for at least a year. If you are selected, you serve for the duration of that one trial only.</p>
<p>The state pulls potential jurors from multiple sources: voter registration rolls, driver's license records, and state ID databases. This broader net means Florida catches more residents than states relying solely on voter registration — so don't think you'll slip through the cracks by not voting.</p>
<h3>Timeline After Moving</h3>
<p>Your name enters the jury pool system within 30-60 days of establishing Florida residency. This happens automatically when you register to vote or get a Florida driver's license — both requirements you should complete within 30 days of moving here anyway. Check out our <a href="/moving-checklist/">complete moving checklist</a> for all the timing requirements.</p>
<p>Most counties use a random computer selection process that pulls names 6-8 weeks before the service date. So if you moved to Florida in January, don't be surprised to get a summons by April or May.</p>
<h2>Understanding Your Summons</h2>
<p>Florida jury summons arrive by regular mail to your registered address — another reason to update your voter registration promptly after moving. The summons includes your reporting date, time, courthouse location, and a unique juror identification number.</p>
<p><strong>Key details you'll find:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Reporting date (typically 8:00 AM)</li>
<li>Courthouse address with parking information</li>
<li>Length of service period (usually one week)</li>
<li>Phone number to call the night before for updates</li>
<li>Instructions for postponements or excusals</li>
</ul>
<h3>What That Phone Number Actually Does</h3>
<p>Most Florida counties use an automated system that updates daily by 6:00 PM. You'll call using your juror ID number to learn if your group needs to report the next day. Sometimes entire groups get cancelled due to plea bargains or settled cases — this happens more often than you'd expect.</p>
<h2>Who Gets Excused and Who Doesn't</h2>
<p>Florida's exemption list is shorter than many states, but here's what actually works:</p>
<h3>Automatic Disqualifications</h3>
<ul>
<li>Not a U.S. citizen</li>
<li>Not a Florida resident</li>
<li>Under 18 years old</li>
<li>Convicted of a felony (unless rights restored)</li>
<li>Currently under prosecution for a crime</li>
</ul>
<h3>Valid Excuses (Must Be Requested)</h3>
<ul>
<li>Over 70 years old (automatic excuse if requested)</li>
<li>Primary caregiver for someone unable to care for themselves</li>
<li>Full-time student during academic term</li>
<li>Sole caretaker of children under 6 with no childcare available</li>
<li>Law enforcement officers, firefighters, EMTs on active duty</li>
<li>Elected officials during their term</li>
</ul>
<h3>What Doesn't Work</h3>
<p>Florida courts rarely accept these common attempts:</p>
<ul>
<li>"I already have vacation planned"</li>
<li>"I'm too busy at work"</li>
<li>"I don't believe in the legal system"</li>
<li>"I watch too much Law &#x26; Order to be impartial"</li>
</ul>
<p>The courts have heard every excuse. Stick to legitimate hardships with documentation.</p>
<h2>The Money Situation — What You'll Actually Earn</h2>
<p>Florida pays jurors $15 per day starting on the second day of service. Your first day is unpaid. If selected for a trial lasting multiple days, you receive $15 for each day beyond the first.</p>
<h3>Breaking Down the Numbers</h3>
<ul>
<li>Day 1: $0</li>
<li>Days 2-3: $15 per day</li>
<li>Day 4 and beyond: $30 per day</li>
</ul>
<p>Plus mileage reimbursement of $0.445 per mile from your home to the courthouse (2024 rate). For most Tampa Bay residents, this adds $3-8 to your daily total depending on which courthouse you're assigned.</p>
<p><strong>Reality check:</strong> This doesn't cover your actual costs. If you're hourly, you're likely losing money. If you're salaried with paid jury leave, you're ahead. Many employers are required to continue paying during jury service, but Florida law only protects you from being fired — not from losing pay.</p>
<h2>The Day-of-Service Experience</h2>
<p>Plan to arrive 30 minutes early for parking and security screening. Most Florida courthouses have metal detectors and bag checks similar to airports. Bring a book, your phone charger, and patience.</p>
<h3>What Actually Happens</h3>
<p><strong>8:00 AM:</strong> Check in with courthouse staff
<strong>8:30 AM:</strong> Orientation video (usually 15-20 minutes of civics refresher)
<strong>9:00 AM:</strong> First round of potential jury selections called
<strong>Throughout the day:</strong> Additional panels called as needed</p>
<p>You'll spend most time in the jury assembly room — essentially a waiting room with uncomfortable chairs and a TV usually tuned to morning shows. Some courthouses have WiFi; many don't. Cellular service can be spotty in government buildings.</p>
<h3>If Your Number Gets Called</h3>
<p>You'll move to a specific courtroom for "voir dire" — the selection process where attorneys ask questions to determine if you're suitable for their case. This isn't a test with right or wrong answers. Answer honestly.</p>
<p>Common questions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Have you been a victim of similar crimes?</li>
<li>Do you know anyone involved in the case?</li>
<li>Can you be fair and impartial?</li>
<li>Have you served on juries before?</li>
</ul>
<p>Attorneys can dismiss potential jurors "for cause" (bias or inability to serve) or use "peremptory challenges" (no reason needed, but limited number allowed).</p>
<h2>Tampa Bay Specific Courthouses</h2>
<p>Different types of cases get heard in different courts, and you'll be summoned based on your address:</p>
<h3>Hillsborough County (Tampa)</h3>
<p><strong>Main Courthouse:</strong> 800 E Twiggs St, Tampa
<strong>Jury parking:</strong> $5 per day in courthouse garage
<strong>What they hear:</strong> Felony criminal, major civil cases</p>
<p><strong>Edgecomb Courthouse:</strong> 419 Pierce St, Tampa
<strong>Focus:</strong> County court matters, smaller civil cases
<strong>Parking:</strong> Metered street parking or nearby lots</p>
<h3>Pinellas County (St. Petersburg/Clearwater)</h3>
<p><strong>Criminal Justice Center:</strong> 14250 49th St N, Clearwater
<strong>Parking:</strong> Free lot available
<strong>Specialty:</strong> Major criminal cases</p>
<p><strong>St. Petersburg Courthouse:</strong> 545 1st Ave N, St. Petersburg
<strong>Civil focus:</strong> Personal injury, contract disputes
<strong>Parking:</strong> Paid downtown lots, limited street parking</p>
<p>Check your summons carefully for the correct location — showing up to the wrong courthouse doesn't count as compliance.</p>
<h2>Strategies for Managing Jury Duty</h2>
<h3>Before You Go</h3>
<ul>
<li>Confirm your service date by calling the automated line</li>
<li>Arrange childcare or pet care for potential multi-day service</li>
<li>Notify your employer as soon as you receive the summons</li>
<li>Pack snacks and entertainment (but check courthouse rules first)</li>
</ul>
<h3>During Service</h3>
<ul>
<li>Dress professionally but comfortably — you might sit for hours</li>
<li>Bring layers — courthouse temperatures vary wildly</li>
<li>Keep an open mind during voir dire questioning</li>
<li>Follow all court instructions precisely</li>
</ul>
<h3>Postponement Options</h3>
<p>Florida allows one postponement to a later date within the same jury term (usually the same quarter). You must request this in writing before your scheduled date. Valid reasons include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Pre-planned vacation</li>
<li>Medical appointments</li>
<li>Work conflicts with advance notice</li>
<li>Family emergencies</li>
</ul>
<h2>What Happens If You Ignore It</h2>
<p>Don't. Florida treats failure to appear for jury duty as contempt of court. Penalties include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fines up to $100 for first offense</li>
<li>Community service hours</li>
<li>Possible arrest warrant for repeated non-compliance</li>
</ul>
<p>The courts track summons delivery through the postal service. "I never got it" rarely works, especially if you've moved without updating your voter registration or driver's license address.</p>
<h3>The Exception</h3>
<p>If you genuinely didn't receive the summons due to mail forwarding delays after a recent move, contact the clerk's office immediately with proof of your address change timing. Most courts will work with recent transplants on legitimate timing conflicts.</p>
<h2>Types of Cases You Might See</h2>
<p>Florida jury service exposes you to the full range of legal disputes:</p>
<h3>Criminal Cases</h3>
<ul>
<li>DUI charges (very common in Florida)</li>
<li>Theft and burglary</li>
<li>Drug possession or trafficking</li>
<li>Assault and domestic violence</li>
<li>White-collar crimes</li>
</ul>
<h3>Civil Cases</h3>
<ul>
<li>Personal injury (car accidents, slip-and-falls)</li>
<li>Contract disputes</li>
<li>Property damage claims</li>
<li>Medical malpractice</li>
<li>Employment discrimination</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Trial length:</strong> Most cases resolve in 1-3 days. Complex civil cases or serious felonies might run a week or longer, but you'll know this upfront during selection.</p>
<h2>Making the Most of It</h2>
<p>Jury duty offers a unique glimpse into how Florida's legal system actually works. You'll see real attorneys, real judges, and real cases affecting your community. Many people find it more interesting than expected — once they get past the initial inconvenience.</p>
<p>Plus, serving once means you're exempt for at least 12 months in most Florida counties. Some counties provide longer exemption periods, especially if you serve on a lengthy trial.</p>

<h2>Pro Tips From a Long-Time Florida Resident</h2>
<p>After watching hundreds of clients navigate Florida jury duty over the years, here's what actually makes a difference:</p>
<h3>Update Your Address Immediately</h3>
<p>The single biggest headache I see involves people getting summoned to their old county after moving within Florida. The computer systems don't always sync quickly between counties. Update your voter registration and driver's license within 30 days of any move — not just when you first arrive in Florida.</p>
<h3>Know Your Employer's Policy</h3>
<p>Florida doesn't require employers to pay wages during jury service, though many do. Find out your company's policy before you're summoned. Some employers treat jury duty as paid civic leave; others make you use vacation days or go unpaid.</p>
<h3>Consider the Timing</h3>
<p>If you receive a summons during a particularly busy work period, remember you get one postponement opportunity. Use it strategically rather than hoping to get excused on the day of service.</p>
<h3>Bring Cash for Parking</h3>
<p>Most courthouses charge for parking, and not all accept credit cards. Downtown Tampa and St. Petersburg meters require coins or apps. Budget $5-10 for parking unless you're at a courthouse with free lots.</p>
<h2>The Reality Check Nobody Mentions</h2>
<p>Here's what jury duty guidebooks don't tell you: Most people called for jury service never actually serve on a jury. Between cases settling at the last minute, having too many potential jurors show up, and the selection process eliminating people for various reasons, your odds of spending the day in the jury room reading magazines are pretty high.</p>
<p>But if you do get selected, take it seriously. You're making decisions that genuinely affect people's lives, whether it's a civil case involving someone's financial future or a criminal case that could result in jail time.</p>
<p>The attorneys and judges can tell the difference between jurors who are engaged and those who just want to go home. Be the juror you'd want deciding your case.</p>
<h2>After Your Service</h2>
<p>Once you complete jury duty — whether you served on a trial or just spent the day waiting — you'll receive a certificate of completion. Keep this for your records. Some employers require proof of service for payroll purposes.</p>
<p>Your name goes back into the jury pool system, but with a flag indicating recent service. You won't be called again for at least 12 months, and in most Florida counties, it's typically 2-3 years before you see another summons.</p>
<p>If you served on a particularly long trial (more than a week), some courts provide longer exemption periods. The clerk's office can confirm your specific exemption end date.</p>
<h2>Planning Around Florida's System</h2>
<p>Unlike states that call jurors for specific trials, Florida's broader pool system means less predictability about actual service length. When you get summoned, assume you might be selected and plan accordingly:</p>
<ul>
<li>Arrange flexible work coverage for the entire week</li>
<li>Have backup childcare options</li>
<li>Don't schedule important appointments during your jury week</li>
<li>Budget for parking and meal costs if selected for trial</li>
</ul>
<p>The "one day, one trial" system works in your favor most of the time, but when you do get picked for a multi-day trial, you'll be glad you planned ahead.</p>
<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>Can I be excused from jury duty if I just moved to Florida?</h3>
<p>No, recent relocation doesn't qualify as an excuse. Once you establish Florida residency (register to vote or get a Florida license), you're eligible for jury service. However, if you received a summons before updating your address and there's documentation proving the timing conflict, contact the clerk's office — they may work with you on rescheduling.</p>
<h3>How much notice do I get before reporting for jury duty?</h3>
<p>Florida courts typically send summons 6-8 weeks before your service date. The summons arrives by regular mail, so make sure your voter registration and driver's license have your current address to avoid missing the notice.</p>
<h3>What happens if my employer won't let me serve on jury duty?</h3>
<p>Florida law prohibits employers from firing employees for jury service, but doesn't require them to pay wages. If your employer threatens termination or retaliation for serving, document everything and contact the clerk's office — they can provide information about legal protections.</p>
<h3>Can I bring my phone and laptop to the courthouse?</h3>
<p>Most Florida courthouses allow phones but restrict their use in courtrooms during proceedings. Laptops and tablets may be permitted in jury waiting areas but prohibited in actual courtrooms. Check your specific courthouse rules — policies vary by county and some require devices to be turned off completely.</p>
<h3>How long do trials typically last in Florida?</h3>
<p>Most trials last 1-3 days. Simple civil cases might resolve in one day, while complex criminal cases could run a week or longer. During jury selection, the judge will estimate trial length so you know what you're committing to if selected.</p>
<h3>What if I have a medical condition that makes jury service difficult?</h3>
<p>Medical conditions may qualify you for excuse or accommodation, but you need documentation from your doctor. Submit requests in writing before your service date. Courts can often accommodate mobility issues, hearing problems, or other conditions rather than excusing you entirely.</p>
<h3>Do I get paid for jury duty if I'm unemployed or retired?</h3>
<p>Yes, Florida's jury pay applies regardless of employment status. You receive $15 per day starting on the second day of service, plus mileage reimbursement. The payment is taxable income, and you'll receive a 1099 form if you serve multiple days.</p>
<h3>Can I request a specific courthouse if there are multiple locations in my county?</h3>
<p>No, courthouse assignment is based on your home address and the type of case. You can't choose locations for convenience, but you can request one postponement to a different date within the same service period if the assigned courthouse creates legitimate hardship.</p>
