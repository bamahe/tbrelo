<h2>Water: More Than You Think, Smarter Than You'd Guess</h2>
<p>The standard advice is one gallon per person per day. That's for drinking, basic hygiene, and cooking. For a family of four prepping for a week-long outage, you're looking at 28 gallons minimum. Good luck fitting that in your pantry.</p>
<p><strong>The Smart Water Strategy:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Fill every container you own before the storm hits: pots, pitchers, that massive stockpot you never use</li>
<li>Fill your bathtub(s) — that's 30-50 gallons for non-drinking needs</li>
<li>Buy a <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B001AXLUX2%7D%7D">WaterBOB bathtub water storage system</a> — it holds 100 gallons and keeps it clean</li>
<li>Stock 3-4 cases of bottled water for drinking</li>
<li>Keep water purification tablets as backup: <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B001949TKS%7D%7D">Potable Aqua tablets</a></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pro tip from Hillsborough County:</strong> Your hot water heater holds 40-80 gallons of drinkable water. Learn how to drain it safely before you need to.</p>
<p>Don't forget about your pets. A 70-pound dog needs about a gallon every three days. Your cat? About a cup daily. Plan accordingly.</p>
<h2>Power: Generators, Batteries, and Reality Checks</h2>
<p>Let's talk generators. The 3,500-watt Honda everyone recommends costs $3,000 and will power your refrigerator, a few lights, and charge your phones. It won't run your AC, electric stove, or pool pump. Know what you're buying.</p>
<p><strong>Generator Sizing Reality Check:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Refrigerator: 600-800 watts</li>
<li>Window AC unit: 1,200-1,500 watts</li>
<li>LED lights (10 bulbs): 100 watts</li>
<li>Phone chargers: 25 watts each</li>
<li>Coffee maker: 1,000 watts (priorities, people)</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Better Generator Strategy:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li><a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B07FKXCHQD%7D%7D">Champion 4000-watt dual fuel</a> — runs on gas or propane, quieter than most</li>
<li>Buy propane tanks, not just gas — propane stores longer and burns cleaner</li>
<li>Get a transfer switch installed ($500-800) so you're not running extension cords through windows</li>
<li>Practice setting it up before the storm. I've watched neighbors struggle with their brand-new generator while the eye wall approached.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>For Apartments/Condos:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B084BYCQWK%7D%7D">Jackery portable power station</a> — no gas, no exhaust, charges phones and runs small appliances</li>
<li>Multiple phone battery packs: <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B01JIWQPMW%7D%7D">Anker PowerCore 26800</a></li>
<li>Solar phone chargers for extended outages</li>
</ul>

<h2>Food: Forget the Bread, Think Long Game</h2>
<p>The great bread and milk rush is Florida's most pointless tradition. Unless you're planning to make french toast by candlelight, skip the dairy aisle madness and think strategically.</p>
<p><strong>Hurricane Food That Actually Makes Sense:</strong></p>
<p><strong>Protein That Doesn't Spoil:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Canned chicken, tuna, salmon</li>
<li>Peanut butter (lots of it)</li>
<li>Protein bars</li>
<li>Dried beans and lentils (if you have camp stove)</li>
<li>Nuts and seeds</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Easy Carbs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Crackers (not bread that goes moldy in humidity)</li>
<li>Pasta (if you can boil water)</li>
<li>Rice (ditto)</li>
<li>Oatmeal packets</li>
<li>Granola bars</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Fruits and Vegetables:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Canned fruits in juice, not syrup</li>
<li>Applesauce pouches</li>
<li>Dried fruits</li>
<li>Canned vegetables (eat cold if needed)</li>
<li>V8 juice for vitamins</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The Hurricane Cooking Reality:</strong>
Most people lose power and can't cook anything that requires electricity. A small camp stove like the <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B0009PURJA%7D%7D">Coleman Butane Stove</a> lets you boil water and heat simple meals. Butane canisters are easier to store than propane tanks.</p>
<p>Don't forget a manual can opener. Sounds obvious until you're holding a can of beans and staring at your electric opener.</p>
<h2>Shelter: Windows, Doors, and Sanity</h2>
<p><strong>Hurricane Shutters — Do It Right or Don't Do It:</strong>
Plywood is cheap and heavy and takes forever to install when you're racing a storm. If you own your home in <a href="/counties/hillsborough/">Hillsborough</a>, <a href="/counties/pinellas/">Pinellas</a>, or <a href="/counties/pasco/">Pasco County</a>, invest in proper protection:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Accordion shutters:</strong> $15-25 per square foot, permanent installation</li>
<li><strong>Roll-down shutters:</strong> $20-35 per square foot, best protection but priciest</li>
<li><strong>Storm panels:</strong> $7-12 per square foot, removable aluminum or steel</li>
<li><strong>Impact windows:</strong> $40-55 per square foot, permanent protection plus insurance discounts</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>For Renters:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>5/8" plywood minimum, not the 1/2" stuff that'll bow</li>
<li>Pre-cut and label each piece</li>
<li>Store screws in labeled bags</li>
<li>Practice installation — it takes longer than you think</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Secondary Shelter Needs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Tarps for roof damage: <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B07GDCQZXF%7D%7D">heavy-duty 10x12 tarp</a></li>
<li>Duct tape (the real silver stuff, not cheap)</li>
<li>Plastic sheeting for broken windows</li>
<li>Work gloves for cleanup</li>
</ul>
<h2>Communications: When Cell Towers Go Dark</h2>
<p>Your iPhone won't help when the cell towers lose power. Plan for communication blackouts that can last days.</p>
<p><strong>Essential Communication Gear:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Battery/solar radio:</strong> <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B01MAWEN5W%7D%7D">Eton FRX5-BT</a> gets weather alerts and AM/FM</li>
<li><strong>Two-way radios:</strong> <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B001WMFYH4%7D%7D">Midland GXT1000VP4</a> for family communication (2+ mile range)</li>
<li><strong>Satellite communicator:</strong> <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B07CR21Y6G%7D%7D">Garmin inReach Mini</a> for real emergencies</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Important Papers in Waterproof Storage:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Insurance policies (photo them too)</li>
<li>Birth certificates, passports, social security cards</li>
<li>Property deeds/mortgage papers</li>
<li>Medical prescriptions and records</li>
<li>Bank account information</li>
<li>Emergency contact lists (written down — not just in your phone)</li>
</ul>
<p>Store physical copies in a fireproof, waterproof safe like the <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B000PXM0FE%7D%7D">SentrySafe H3300</a>.</p>
<h2>Medical and Hygiene: The Unsexy Essentials</h2>
<p><strong>Prescription Medications:</strong>
Call your pharmacy two weeks before hurricane season starts. Get 90-day supplies of critical medications. Most insurance plans allow early refills for disaster prep.</p>
<p><strong>First Aid Beyond Band-Aids:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Antiseptic wipes and cream</li>
<li>Gauze pads and medical tape</li>
<li>Ibuprofen and acetaminophen</li>
<li>Antacid tablets</li>
<li>Anti-diarrheal medication</li>
<li>Thermometer (non-digital)</li>
<li>Emergency inhaler if anyone has asthma</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Hygiene Without Running Water:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Baby wipes (lots of them)</li>
<li>Dry shampoo</li>
<li>Toilet paper (more than you think)</li>
<li>Feminine hygiene products</li>
<li>Toothbrushes and toothpaste</li>
<li>Hand sanitizer</li>
<li>Soap that works in cold water</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Special Needs:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Baby formula and diapers (if applicable)</li>
<li>Pet food and medications</li>
<li>Hearing aid batteries</li>
<li>Glasses/contact lens supplies</li>
</ul>
<h2>Tools and Safety: When Everything Breaks</h2>
<p><strong>Essential Hurricane Tools:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Chainsaw or battery reciprocating saw:</strong> For clearing fallen trees. <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B077Q2NNXL%7D%7D">Echo CS-310</a> is reliable and not too heavy</li>
<li><strong>Axe and hand saw:</strong> When the chainsaw won't start</li>
<li><strong>Heavy-duty flashlights:</strong> <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B00B8Q31UQ%7D%7D">Streamlight Siege</a> lanterns light up rooms</li>
<li><strong>Headlamps:</strong> Keep your hands free. <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B098T7JJ2R%7D%7D">Petzl Tikka</a></li>
<li><strong>Multi-tool:</strong> <a href="%7B%7Bamazon:B078WBQZPX%7D%7D">Leatherman Wave+</a></li>
<li><strong>Crowbar/pry bar:</strong> For removing debris</li>
<li><strong>Shovel:</strong> You'll need it</li>
<li><strong>Work gloves:</strong> Heavy leather, not garden gloves</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Safety Equipment:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Carbon monoxide detectors:</strong> Battery-powered ones for generator use</li>
<li><strong>Fire extinguisher:</strong> Small one for camp stove accidents</li>
<li><strong>Emergency whistle:</strong> If you're trapped</li>
<li><strong>Rope:</strong> 50 feet of 1/2" nylon rope</li>
<li><strong>Plastic sheeting:</strong> 6-mil thickness minimum</li>
</ul>
<h2>Financial Preparation: Cash and Cards</h2>
<p>ATMs don't work without power. Credit card machines need internet. Keep $500-1000 in small bills ($1s, $5s, $10s, $20s) for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Gas stations that reopen first (often cash-only)</li>
<li>Ice vendors</li>
<li>Food trucks</li>
<li>Contractors for emergency repairs</li>
<li>Tips for hotel staff if you evacuate</li>
</ul>
<p>Photograph or scan important financial documents:</p>
<ul>
<li>Insurance cards</li>
<li>Driver's licenses</li>
<li>Credit cards (front and back)</li>
<li>Bank account information</li>
</ul>
<p>Store copies in cloud storage and on a USB drive.</p>
<h2>Regional Specifics: Tampa Bay Hurricane Realities</h2>
<p><strong>Pinellas County Residents:</strong>
You're on a peninsula. Evacuation zones are serious business. If you're in Zone A (anywhere near the water), you're leaving when they say leave. Period. The Courtney Campbell Causeway and Howard Frankland Bridge will be packed.</p>
<p><strong>Hillsborough County:</strong>
Inland flooding is your biggest threat after wind damage. Areas like <a href="/counties/hillsborough/">Temple Terrace and Brandon</a> can flood from river overflow days after the storm passes. Know your evacuation zone and flood zone — they're different things.</p>
<p><strong>Pasco County:</strong>
You're far enough inland that storm surge isn't the primary concern, but you'll get the full wind impact. Power outages can last longer because you're not the priority for restoration. Plan accordingly.</p>
<p><strong>Hurricane Kit Storage:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Store supplies in multiple locations (garage, house, car trunk)</li>
<li>Rotate perishables every six months</li>
<li>Test equipment annually</li>
<li>Keep inventory list updated</li>
</ul>

<h2>The 72-Hour Rule (And Why It's Wrong)</h2>
<p>Emergency management loves to say "be prepared for 72 hours." That's optimistic. Hurricane Ian left some Southwest Florida areas without power for three weeks. Hurricane Charley in 2004 had Punta Gorda dark for 10 days.</p>
<p>Plan for a week minimum. Two weeks if you really want to sleep well at night.</p>
<p><strong>Week 1 Priorities:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Safety and shelter</li>
<li>Water and basic food</li>
<li>Communication with family</li>
<li>Medical needs</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Week 2 Realities:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Spoiled food cleanup</li>
<li>Insurance claims</li>
<li>Contractor scams</li>
<li>Price gouging</li>
<li>Gas shortages</li>
<li>Hotel booking if home is damaged</li>
</ul>
<h2>What NOT to Buy (Despite What Everyone Says)</h2>
<p><strong>Skip These Hurricane "Essentials":</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tons of bread:</strong> Goes bad fast in humidity</li>
<li><strong>Milk:</strong> Spoils without refrigeration</li>
<li><strong>Frozen foods:</strong> Unless you have generator power for your freezer</li>
<li><strong>Glass containers:</strong> Break easily, create hazards</li>
<li><strong>Matches:</strong> Get waterproof ones or lighters</li>
<li><strong>Cheap batteries:</strong> Kirkland batteries from Costco outperform most name brands</li>
<li><strong>Single-use items:</strong> Think reusable when possible</li>
</ul>
<h2>The Psychology of Hurricane Prep</h2>
<p>Here's what nobody talks about: hurricane prep is as much mental as physical. You're preparing for an event that might not happen, spending money on supplies you hope to never use, all while trying to maintain normal life.</p>
<p><strong>Mental Preparation Strategies:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Start your list in May, not August</li>
<li>Buy a few items each grocery trip</li>
<li>Don't wait for a storm to test your generator</li>
<li>Practice your evacuation route during non-emergency times</li>
<li>Know your neighbors — you'll need each other</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Kids and Hurricanes:</strong>
Make it an adventure, not a panic. Let them help with prep. Explain what's happening age-appropriately. Pack extra entertainment: coloring books, cards, board games. Scared kids make everything harder.</p>
<h2>After the Storm: The Real Test</h2>
<p>Your hurricane kit doesn't end when the winds stop. The real test starts during recovery:</p>
<p><strong>Immediate Post-Storm (First 24 Hours):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Stay inside until officials say it's safe</li>
<li>Check for gas leaks (smell)</li>
<li>Turn off main electrical breaker if there's flooding</li>
<li>Document damage with photos</li>
<li>Check on neighbors</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>First Week Recovery:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Avoid standing water (could be electrified)</li>
<li>Don't use generators indoors or in garages</li>
<li>Boil water if authorities recommend it</li>
<li>Keep receipts for everything</li>
<li>Be patient with insurance adjusters</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Contractor Warning:</strong>
Storm chasers show up fast with out-of-state plates and cash-only demands. Get multiple estimates. Check licenses. Never pay everything upfront. Legitimate contractors understand insurance processes.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<h3>How much should I spend on a complete hurricane kit?</h3>
<p>A basic kit for a family of four runs $800-1,200, including a mid-range generator. Spread purchases over several months to avoid sticker shock. Quality items like generators and shutters pay for themselves over time through insurance discounts and peace of mind.</p>
<h3>When should I start shopping for hurricane supplies?</h3>
<p>Start in May, finish by July. Waiting until a storm is named means fighting crowds and paying premium prices. Most items don't expire, so early shopping just makes sense.</p>
<h3>Do I really need a generator if I live in an apartment?</h3>
<p>Not a gas generator, but a large battery pack like a Jackery can keep phones charged and run small fans for days. Many apartment complexes have community generators for common areas. Focus on portable solutions that don't require permanent installation.</p>
<h3>How do I know what evacuation zone I'm in?</h3>
<p>Check your county's emergency management website. In <a href="/counties/hillsborough/">Hillsborough County</a>, zones run A through E, with A being highest risk. Your zone determines when you should evacuate, not whether you should evacuate.</p>
<h3>Should I evacuate or ride out the storm?</h3>
<p>If you're in an evacuation zone, evacuate when officials tell you to. If you're not in an evacuation zone but feel unsafe, leave anyway. Emergency responders can't help you during the storm, so don't count on rescue if you change your mind.</p>
<h3>What's the biggest mistake people make with hurricane prep?</h3>
<p>Waiting too long and then panicking. The families who do best start preparing in May, not when a storm is 72 hours out. Second biggest mistake: buying cheap equipment that fails when you need it most. This isn't the time to save money on batteries or flashlights.</p>
